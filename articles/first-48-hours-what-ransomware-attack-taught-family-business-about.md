## At a glance
G&H Distributing, a family‑owned distributor based in Sioux Falls, lost access to its ERP and file servers within minutes of a ransomware intrusion [[1](#ref-1)]. The incident shows how quickly a small organization can become unable to operate, and why response planning is as critical as prevention.

## What happened
On a typical weekday, employees discovered that critical systems were inaccessible and files encrypted, confirming a ransomware infection [[1](#ref-1)]. The attack halted order processing, inventory management, and financial reporting, leaving the business effectively dead in the water [[1](#ref-1)]. The company's IT provider, Eide Bailly, immediately isolated the compromised assets and began incident‑response procedures, but the first 48 hours were dominated by uncertainty and urgent decision‑making [[1](#ref-1)].

Ransomware remains a dominant threat to small enterprises. A recent analysis shows that 43 % of all ransomware victims are small businesses, and attacks have risen 300 % in the past year alone [[2](#ref-2)]. The financial impact can be severe: average ransom demands exceeded $200,000 in 2023, and total costs (including downtime, recovery, and reputational damage) often reach $4.6 million per incident [[2](#ref-2)].

The attackers employed a ransomware family that encrypts data and drops a ransom note, a tactic common among modern ransomware‑as‑a‑service (RaaS) operators [[4](#ref-4)]. While the specific family was not disclosed, the incident aligns with the pattern of RaaS groups that provide the malicious payload to affiliates, who then negotiate the ransom [[4](#ref-4)].

## Why it matters
Small businesses form the backbone of many economies, yet they frequently lack robust cybersecurity defenses. In the United States, small firms accounted for 50 % or more of ransomware incidents in 2021, and they now represent roughly half to three‑quarters of all victims [[5](#ref-5)]. The surge in attacks is not limited to the U.S.; India's expanding digital footprint has also increased exposure, prompting warnings that ransomware risk is becoming a systemic business risk [[3](#ref-3)].

The consequences extend beyond immediate financial loss. Operational disruption can erode customer trust, jeopardize supply chains, and even trigger national‑level emergencies when critical infrastructure is affected [[6](#ref-6)]. For a family business, the stakes include the livelihood of owners, employees, and the community that depends on its services.

## Technical details
Ransomware families share common code signatures, payloads, and attack playbooks, enabling affiliates to launch sophisticated campaigns with minimal effort [[4](#ref-4)]. The malware typically gains entry through phishing emails, compromised credentials, or unpatched software, then moves laterally across the network to locate high‑value data [[4](#ref-4)]. Once files are encrypted, the attackers present a ransom note that identifies the family and provides payment instructions, which incident responders use to triage the event [[4](#ref-4)].

Detection and response are complicated by the sheer number of families (approximately 250 are known) each with multiple variants [[4](#ref-4)]. Security tools that rely on signature‑based detection must be continuously updated, while behavioral analytics can flag anomalous encryption activity before files are fully locked [[4](#ref-4)].

## What defenders should do
1. **Develop an incident‑response plan** that outlines roles, communication channels, and decision criteria for ransom negotiations [[5](#ref-5)][[6](#ref-6)].
2. **Implement layered defenses**: regular patch management, multi‑factor authentication, email filtering, and endpoint detection and response (EDR) solutions [[5](#ref-5)][[6](#ref-6)].
3. **Conduct frequent backups** stored offline or in immutable cloud buckets, ensuring recovery without paying a ransom [[5](#ref-5)][[6](#ref-6)].
4. **Perform regular security assessments** to identify gaps in network segmentation, privileged‑access controls, and third‑party vendor risk [[5](#ref-5)][[6](#ref-6)].
5. **Educate employees** on phishing awareness and safe handling of attachments, reducing the likelihood of initial compromise [[5](#ref-5)][[6](#ref-6)].

A proactive posture (combining prevention, detection, and response) significantly lowers the probability of a successful ransomware event and shortens recovery time if an attack does occur.
