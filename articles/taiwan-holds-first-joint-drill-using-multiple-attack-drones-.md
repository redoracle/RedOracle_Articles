# Taiwan Holds First Joint Drill Using Multiple Attack Drones as Modernisation Push Intensifies

## At a glance

On September 18, 2026, Taiwan carried out its first joint combat drill featuring multiple attack systems—a clear marker in the country's ongoing modernization push [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)]. The three-day exercise unfolded in Southern Taiwan and displayed both domestic and allied capabilities, including Hsiung Feng III anti-ship missiles and U.S. M142 HIMARS rocket systems [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)]. Most significantly, President William Lai Ching-te personally visited the live-fire missile drills [[4](#ref-4)]. This multi-drone, multi-system drill highlights Taiwan's growing integration of homegrown defense technologies with international partners as regional pressures mount [[5](#ref-5)].

## What happened

The September 18, 2026 exercise was a comprehensive three-day joint combat drill staged across Southern Taiwan [[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)]. The scenario focused on China-centric anti-invasion operations, weaving together distinct weapon families to simulate realistic threats [[3](#ref-3)]. Indigeneous Hsiung Feng III anti-ship missiles formed a core component, having been developed domestically to engage naval targets [[1](#ref-1)]. These were paired with U.S.-produced M142 HIMARS rocket launchers, introducing American precision-guidance artillery into the mix [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)].

The drill brought together a broad array of attack assets working in concert. Covered by media, participants integrated HIMARS launchers, unmanned systems, and missiles into a coordinated simulation designed to measure response times and assess interoperability among different weapon families [[3](#ref-3)]. Spanning multiple days allowed for repeated practice and tactical refinement against simulated invasion scenarios [[2](#ref-2)].

President William Lai Ching-te played a central role, offering direct oversight and personally inspecting the joint missile live-fire demonstrations [[4](#ref-4)]. His presence underscored governmental commitment and reinforced the symbolic weight of domestic leadership in national security matters [[4](#ref-4)].

## Why it matters

The March 18, 2026 event represents Taiwan's largest-scale joint drill that integrates multiple attack drone and missile systems simultaneously [[1](#ref-1)]. Such an exercise showcases the island's expanding ability to fuse diverse technological assets—from domestically developed ballistic missiles to cutting-edge U.S. ground-based tactical systems—as part of a broader modernization strategy [[5](#ref-5)].

Strategically, the drill signals Taiwan's evolving defense posture amid intensifying Chinese military activity along its perimeter [[5](#ref-5)]. By executing concurrent deployments of Hsiung Feng III sea-to-sea strike capability and HIMARS precision firepower, the armed forces tested their capacity to respond to threats spanning air, land, and maritime domains [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)].

The multi-system approach also reflects Taiwan's effort to diversify and strengthen its deterrent architecture. The indigenous development of the Hsiung Feng III parallels models used worldwide, where nations pursue self-reliance while still leveraging allied technology when needed [[3](#ref-3)]. The recent drill therefore embodies not only technical modernization but also a deliberate strategy for crisis preparedness [[5](#ref-5)].

Moreover, the public nature of the drill—highlighted by President Lai Ching-te's active involvement—carries diplomatic weight. Demonstrating joint operations with both domestic and foreign systems bolsters regional credibility and shows allies the effectiveness of Taiwan's defensive readiness [[4](#ref-4)]. As tensions persist, these transparent displays of capability serve as vital tools in shaping perceptions and partnerships [[5](#ref-5)].

In sum, the September 18, 2026 joint drill marks a tangible milestone in Taiwan's defense evolution. By pairing indigenous capabilities with allied systems in a realistic, multi-drone, multi-missile exercise, the military demonstrated operational maturity while signaling adaptability to emerging threats [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)]. For a nation under constant external pressure, such exercises are far from routine—they are essential elements of a sustained modernization push aimed at securing sovereignty [[5](#ref-5)].
