# FBI Warns of Hackers Targeting Grocery Store Fridges, Freezers

## At a glance

The FBI has issued a public warning about malicious cyber activity targeting industrial refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems at grocery stores and cold storage facilities across Europe, with authorities urging affected operations to isolate and secure vulnerable controllers [[1](#ref-1)]. Hackers have demonstrated the ability to manipulate temperature settings and activate permanent defrost and heating modes in refrigeration equipment, leading to spoiled food and physical damage to equipment [[2](#ref-2)]. The warnings come as the EU cybersecurity agency ENISA reported a new wave of cases on November 1 and 2, and the FBI, CISA, and multiple U.S. government departments have jointly issued alerts about an ongoing threat to critical infrastructure [[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)]. Former FBI agent Nicole Parker has characterized the attacks as potential psychological warfare that could extend from European supermarkets to U.S. stores [[5](#ref-5)].

## What happened

The FBI issued a public warning on X to grocers and authorities at cold storage facilities about malicious cyber activity targeting industrial refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems [[1](#ref-1)]. Following a series of cyberattacks on European grocery stores in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, Estonia, and France, the agency highlighted how attackers have been able to change temperature settings and switch on permanent defrost and heating modes in refrigeration equipment [[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)]. The alerts describe incidents where hackers gained access to electronic temperature controllers in supermarkets and cold storage sites, then altered internal settings to raise internal temperatures, forcing staff to discard spoiled food [[3](#ref-3)]. In France, attackers targeted the cooling systems at three sites of the national blood transfusion service EFS, where blood plasma was stored; the alarms went off and staff reacted quickly, preventing any loss of blood units [[3](#ref-3)]. Similar outages were reported at about forty Delhaize stores in Belgium and fifteen Carrefour stores in Belgium, as well as at Swedish chains ICA and Coop [[4](#ref-4)]. The attacks have also spread to military bases and a French blood bank, underscoring the breadth of the threat beyond commercial retail [[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)].

## Why it matters

The stakes of these cyberattacks extend far beyond inconvenient spoiled food; they represent a direct threat to food safety and public health [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)]. When refrigeration systems are manipulated to operate at elevated temperatures, perishable goods quickly spoil, creating health risks for consumers and wasting resources [[2](#ref-2)]. Equipment damage is another significant concern, as forced defrost cycles and constant heating can shorten the lifespan of refrigeration units and cause costly repairs [[2](#ref-2)]. The attacks also pose a broader risk to critical infrastructure, since the same industrial refrigeration technologies are used for storing medicine, medical supplies, and other essential products across healthcare, food, and agriculture sectors [[1](#ref-1)][[4](#ref-4)]. Former FBI special agent Nicole Parker warned that such attacks could become a form of psychological warfare, eroding public trust in the food supply chain and potentially destabilizing consumer confidence [[5](#ref-5)]. The potential for similar attacks on U.S. supermarkets, given that the systems are widely deployed and many controllers remain unpatched or exposed to the internet, makes this a pressing national security issue [[5](#ref-5)].

## Technical details

The technical mechanism behind these attacks involves exploiting vulnerabilities in electronic temperature controllers that manage refrigeration equipment [[2](#ref-2)]. Attackers typically gain entry through controllers that are unpatched, reachable from the internet, or still using default passwords listed in manufacturer manuals [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)]. Once inside the system, hackers can change the target temperature of the unit or switch on permanent defrost and heating modes, effectively turning fridges into ovens and freezers into heaters [[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)]. The FBI warned that these temperature manipulations can cause fire hazards, loss of inventory, and accelerated degradation of the refrigeration system itself [[4](#ref-4)]. In some cases, as documented in the French blood bank incident, attackers targeted the cooling systems of blood plasma storage facilities, demonstrating the potential for attacks on life-saving medical supplies [[3](#ref-3)]. The FBI Cyber Branch emphasized that these systems are widely used across critical infrastructure, protecting food and medicine across healthcare, food and agriculture, and other essential sectors in the United States as well [[1](#ref-1)]. The agency advised that attackers could exploit missing updates, unprotected access from the internet, and incorrect settings to gain a foothold in these control systems [[4](#ref-4)].

## What defenders should do

Defenders are urged to take immediate action to isolate and secure refrigeration infrastructure against these threats [[1](#ref-1)][[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)]. The FBI and CISA have recommended that grocers and cold storage operators pull controllers off the internet, apply security patches, and change default credentials to unprotected units [[4](#ref-4)]. Operators should implement network segmentation to prevent attackers from moving laterally from internet-connected systems to industrial control networks [[1](#ref-1)]. The agencies also stress the importance of continuous monitoring and logging of temperature controller activity to detect anomalous behavior such as unexpected temperature changes or mode switches [[4](#ref-4)]. In light of the growing threat, former FBI agent Nicole Parker has called for a heightened sense of vigilance and preparedness, noting that the next attack could target American supermarkets [[5](#ref-5)]. The warning suggests that while the current European attacks have not yet spread to U.S. stores, the attack vectors are similar and the same technologies that protect food and medicine in retail are vulnerable to exploitation [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)].

## What remains unknown

The extent to which the threat has spread to U.S. supermarkets and whether the European attacks were part of a larger, coordinated campaign remains unclear [[5](#ref-5)]. While the FBI has issued joint warnings and alerted authorities across multiple countries, the specific scope of operations in the United States is not fully defined [[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)]. Researchers point to Iran as a potential actor behind the hacks, but the FBI has not publicly confirmed the identity of the cyber actors [[1](#ref-1)]. The investigation is ongoing, and further details about the methods, tools, and motives of the attackers may emerge as more information becomes available [[5](#ref-5)].
