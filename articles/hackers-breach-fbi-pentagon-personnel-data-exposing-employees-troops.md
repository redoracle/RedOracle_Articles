# Hackers breach FBI and Pentagon, exposing millions of personnel records including troops and civilians

## At a glance

This article covers a major cybersecurity incident in which hackers breached the FBI and the Pentagon, exposing the personal records of nearly three million individuals, including active duty service members and civilians. The breach at the Pentagon's Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC) spanned approximately nine months (from mid-October 2025 until its discovery on July 16, 2026) and exposed more than two point seven million living people and about 294,000 deceased. The FBI's job portal was similarly compromised by the ShinyHunters cyber-extortion group, yielding names, addresses, Social Security numbers, medical records, and job details for thousands of agents and millions of personnel. While federal officials have not yet identified a specific perpetrator behind the Pentagon intrusion, the scale of exposure highlights significant vulnerabilities in U.S. government personnel data infrastructure [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)].

## What happened

Hackers struck both the Federal Bureau of Investigation's personnel portal and the Pentagon's central personnel database, the Defense Manpower Data Center (DMDC). The FBI breach was carried out by a group known as ShinyHunters, which infiltrated the FBI's job portal and gained access to sensitive personal information. Affected data included names, physical addresses, Social Security numbers, medical records, and other identifying details for thousands of FBI employees [[1](#ref-1)]. This came on top of ongoing concerns about artificial intelligence and broader cybersecurity threats in the public sector.

The Pentagon breach was more extensive and prolonged. An unauthorized user or group gained access to the DMDC information system between October 2025 and July 2026, a window during which roughly nine million records were stored on unencrypted servers. On July 16, 2026, a security vulnerability was discovered in the file-sharing subsystem, allowing unauthorized users to exfiltrate data. The Defense Manpower Data Center responded by immediately patching the vulnerability and beginning to notify affected individuals. A copy of the official notification letter, dated September 18, 2026, states that the problem was detected in mid-July and has since been remediated [[2](#ref-2)][[4](#ref-4)].

For the Pentagon breach, the scope was staggering. The DMDC manages personnel records for active duty and reserve troops, civilian employees, contractors, retirees, veterans, and their families, collectively more than 60 million records. The exposed dataset comprised approximately 2.76 million living individuals and 294,000 deceased persons. Among the compromised fields were Social Security numbers, dates of birth, contact information, demographic data, and military occupational specialties. Federal officials noted that while no evidence of misuse of the stolen information has been found to date, the combination of personal identifiers and occupational details makes the records particularly sensitive and potentially valuable to malicious actors [[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)].

## Why it matters

The scale of this breach matters because it exposes not only routine personnel records but also highly sensitive classified adjacent information tied to military occupational specialties and job roles. Collin Hogue-Spears, senior director of solutions management at Black Duck, emphasized that the intersection of personal identifiers (such as Social Security numbers) with occupational classifications creates a unique value proposition for adversaries seeking to conduct targeted social engineering, identity theft, or even espionage operations against specific units or individuals [[4](#ref-4)]. This dual layered exposure means that the compromised data could be used to impersonate service members, disrupt base access controls, or facilitate large scale fraud campaigns targeting government employees and contractors.

The operational impact extends beyond immediate identity risks. The Pentagon breach reveals a systemic weakness: the DMDC's file sharing subsystem contained unencrypted Personal Identifiable Information (PII) for roughly nine consecutive months before detection. This suggests inadequate encryption standards and insufficient monitoring of sensitive repositories within critical infrastructure. Such lapses can embolden future attackers and signal gaps in the agency's overall cyber hygiene [[2](#ref-2)][[4](#ref-4)].

From a policy perspective, the exposure of millions of records (including those of living service members and their families) raises serious questions about national security. Service members often represent a strategic asset, and their compensation, career progression, and tactical assignments are protected under law. The same applies to civilian employees whose payroll, health benefits, and employment history are now vulnerable to disclosure [[5](#ref-5)]. Even if the authorities state that misuse has not been observed thus far, the mere availability of such comprehensive data amplifies the risk profile for both individuals and the broader defense establishment.

Finally, the timing (coinciding with heightened scrutiny of AI driven security threats and a surge in ransomware activity) places this incident in the context of a broader trend. The simultaneous compromises at the FBI and Pentagon suggest that the attacker may have exploited common weaknesses across multiple government systems, either through a shared supply chain, a compromised third party vendor, or coordinated attacks leveraging similar exploit techniques [[1](#ref-1)][[3](#ref-3)]. Addressing these cross agency vulnerabilities will require coordinated efforts across the Department of Justice, the Department of Defense, and the national cybersecurity enterprise [[2](#ref-2)][[5](#ref-5)].

In sum, this breach represents a watershed moment for U.S. government data protection. Millions of records spanning nearly three decades of personnel history have been exposed, demanding swift response, enhanced identification infrastructure, and a fundamental reassessment of how sensitive employee and service member data is guarded inside the public sector [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)][[5](#ref-5)].
