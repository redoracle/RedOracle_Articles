# FBI probes second cyberattack this year as hackers breach key crime database, sources say

## At a glance
The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) is investigating a fresh intrusion that compromised the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), a nationwide repository of criminal records, warrants and background‑check data used by federal, state and local police agencies [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)]. The breach represents the second cyber‑incident targeting the bureau this year, following earlier episodes in March and September that involved suspicious activity on internal surveillance systems and a claim by the ShinyHunters group to have stolen employee data [[1](#ref-1)]. While the full scope of the NCIC compromise remains unclear, officials have not disclosed what information, if any, was accessed, and no attribution has been made public [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)]. The episode also coincides with a separate cyber‑attack on a U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) payment system, underscoring heightened concern about the security of critical government databases [[4](#ref-4)].

## What happened
According to multiple reports, the FBI's National Crime Information Center was the target of a cyber‑attack that was discovered and reported over the weekend of November 22 2026 [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)]. The NCIC is described as a "tool officers use to share information on warrants and background checks," and because "so many agencies draw on the system, a breach could matter well beyond the FBI itself" [[1](#ref-1)]. The investigation is being conducted by the FBI, and the agency has not yet released details on the method of intrusion, the data potentially exposed, or the actors responsible [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)][[4](#ref-4)].

President Donald Trump, when asked about the incident during a Fox News interview on Saturday, said he had "only just heard about it," called the news "not good," and added that the government "might learn things it otherwise would not have learned" [[1](#ref-1)].

The breach follows two earlier incidents this year:

* **March**, The FBI disclosed an investigation into suspicious activity on an internal system containing sensitive surveillance and investigative information [[1](#ref-1)].
* **September**, The bureau investigated a breach linked to the criminal hacking group ShinyHunters, which claimed to have stolen data on FBI employees and applicants [[1](#ref-1)].

The latest NCIC incident is the second such event in recent months, and it comes amid a series of technology outages that have affected air travel, banking and other national‑security systems [[4](#ref-4)]. While previous disruptions were attributed to maintenance and technical changes rather than coordinated attacks sponsored by a state, the volume and criticality of the current breaches have raised alarms [[4](#ref-4)].

In response to growing criticism, President Trump recently replaced White House National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross with Kirsten Davies, the Defense Department's chief information officer, signaling an elevated focus on cybersecurity at the highest levels of government [[4](#ref-4)].

## Why it matters
The NCIC is a cornerstone of U.S. law‑enforcement information sharing, linking federal, state and local agencies in real time to support criminal investigations, public safety and inter‑agency cooperation [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)]. A compromise of this database could expose sensitive personal data, undermine trust among partner agencies, and potentially enable malicious actors to manipulate or misuse law‑enforcement records [[1](#ref-1)][[2](#ref-2)][[3](#ref-3)].

The incident also illustrates a broader trend of increasing cyber‑threat activity against U.S. government systems. The FBI itself has been a frequent target; the March and September episodes demonstrate a pattern of repeated intrusions that test the bureau's defensive posture [[1](#ref-1)]. The simultaneous breach of an USDA payment system further highlights the vulnerability of critical infrastructure that underpins essential public services, such as farmer subsidies [[4](#ref-4)].

From an engineering perspective, defending a system like NCIC involves balancing performance, availability and security. The database must remain highly accessible to thousands of authorized users while protecting against unauthorized access, data tampering and denial‑of‑service attacks. Achieving this balance often requires layered controls: strong authentication, encryption at rest and in transit, continuous monitoring, and rapid incident‑response capabilities. The repeated breaches suggest that existing controls may need reinforcement, especially as threat actors adopt more sophisticated techniques.

Finally, the political dimension cannot be ignored. Public statements from President Trump and the appointment of a new cyber‑policy lead indicate that cybersecurity has become a priority at the executive level. How the administration translates that priority into concrete policy, funding and technical guidance will shape the resilience of systems such as NCIC for years to come.

In short, the breach of the National Crime Information Center is not an isolated event; it is a symptom of a larger, evolving threat landscape that challenges the security of core government databases, tests the response capacity of federal agencies, and raises important questions about the adequacy of current cyber‑defenses.
