## Introduction

This article titled “Online Shopping at Work Not a Sackable Offence, UK Judge Rules” examines a recent employment tribunal decision that intersects workplace monitoring, personal use, surveillance, unfair dismissal, employment law, policy, documentation, evidence, tribunal, compensation, two-year-rule. The ruling clarifies how proportionality, contemporaneous documentation, and policy clarity shape outcomes when employers use monitoring tools to detect minor personal use of company equipment.

## Key highlights

- Key finding : Spending under 1 hour and 30 minutes on personal matters across two days is not by itself a sackable offence.
- Subject of the case : Ms A Lanuszka, accountancy administrator at Accountancy MK, dismissed in July 2023 after employer surveillance detected personal computer use.
- Tribunal outcome : Employment Judge Michael Magee ruled the dismissal unfair and awarded Ms Lanuszka more than 14,000 pounds in compensation.
- Context of the ruling : Decision issued following a hearing in Bury St Edmunds, with the ruling published in June 2025.
- Why this matters : Emphasis on proportionality in monitoring and discipline when there is no clearly expressed restrictive policy and when personal use does not interfere with duties.
- Key nuance : Inconsistencies in employer approach, including owner personal use of the same computer and lack of clear policy, undermined the dismissal.

## Context, parties and chronology

- Who : Ms A Lanuszka employed as an administrator at Accountancy MK since 2017. Owner at the time was Ms Krauze. The owner’s sister relocated to the UK and later assumed a more prominent role within the business.
- What : Spyware was installed on Lanuszka’s work computer. Logs showed one hour and 24 minutes of personal use across 13 and 14 July 2023.
- When : Spyware monitoring and dismissal occurred in July 2023. Tribunal ruling issued in June 2025.
- Where : Accountancy MK, UK. Tribunal hearing and ruling delivered by the Employment Tribunal in the Bury St Edmunds area.
- Why this matters : The timing of dismissal coincided with business ownership changes and the owner’s sister moving permanently to the UK, raising questions about motive and possible pretext prior to the two-year unfair dismissal threshold.

## Facts, evidence and policy context

- Personal use versus policy : Lanuszka had no prior conduct problems or formal warnings. The employer did not produce a contemporaneous policy shown to her that expressly prohibited personal use of the work computer.
- Time logged : Spyware recorded 1 hour and 24 minutes of personal use over two days. The judge found a substantial portion of recorded time related to professional development activities such as Excel training.
- Employer behavior : Evidence showed the owner used the computer for personal tasks as well, creating an inconsistent practice environment.
- Evidence quality : Diary entries used by the employer to assert earlier performance discussions were created in 2024 to describe events from 2022 and 2023. The retrospective timing reduced their evidential value and undermined credibility.
- Policy gaps : The absence of a clear, contemporaneous policy on monitoring and permissible personal use amplified the tribunal’s concern about proportionality and fairness.

## Legal reasoning and ruling

- Proportionality and reasonableness : The judge held that limited personal use was not excessive and did not automatically justify summary dismissal.
- Two year service threshold : UK employment law generally gives employees with at least two years of service the right to claim unfair dismissal. The tribunal noted employer actions appeared timed to prevent Ms Lanuszka from reaching that threshold.
- Consistency and fairness : Clean disciplinary record and absence of prior warnings were material factors that weighed against dismissal.
- Contextual factors : Because significant portions of the logged time were for legitimate professional development, and because there was no clear prohibition, the tribunal found dismissal disproportionate.
- Implications for evidence : Reliance on backdated diary entries weakened the employer’s case, highlighting the importance of contemporaneous documentation in disciplinary and monitoring processes.

## Implications for practice and broader significance

- Policy clarity : Employers should establish and communicate explicit policies on permissible personal use of work devices and on monitoring and surveillance.
- Monitoring with intention : Surveillance measures must be proportionate, transparent and supported by timely documentation. Retrospective notes can damage credibility.
- Consistency and fairness : Employers should apply policies consistently and avoid disciplinary steps that appear timed to bypass statutory protections such as the two-year-rule.
- Development versus distraction : Activities recorded as personal may include legitimate professional development. Employers should scrutinize context before treating such activity as misconduct.
- Due process : Employees should be given the opportunity to respond to allegations and employers should document events close to when they occur.

## Practical takeaways for organisations

- Create clear guidance : Draft a concise, widely circulated policy detailing acceptable personal use, monitoring practices and consequences for breaches.
- Train managers in fair procedures : Ensure managers document performance and disciplinary steps contemporaneously and without bias.
- Balance monitoring with trust : Use monitoring tools judiciously and pair them with a transparent rationale that respects employee privacy while protecting business interests.
- Review dismissal triggers : Align disciplinary actions with statutory protections including the two-year-rule and avoid measures aimed at circumventing those protections.
- Document consistently : Maintain contemporaneous notes and avoid creating post hoc diary entries that could undermine credibility.

## Detailed analysis

This section summarises the tribunal reasoning, evidence assessment and the practical legal interactions between monitoring, policy, documentation and unfair dismissal law

- Evidence assessment : Spyware logs provided a raw measure of time spent on personal activity. The tribunal parsed the logs and judgeually distinguished between time spent on browsing and time spent on development related material. The employer’s secondary evidence consisted of diary entries created in 2024. Because those entries were not contemporaneous they carried limited weight. The employer’s inconsistent practice of personal use weakened assertions that the employee had breached a settled standard.
- Proportionality test : The tribunal applied a proportionality lens balancing employer interest in productivity and protecting assets against employee privacy and reasonable personal use during downtime. With only 1 hour and 24 minutes recorded across two days and no prior formal warnings the dismissal was found disproportionate.
- Procedural fairness : The tribunal examined whether the employer followed a fair process including issuing warnings, conducting investigatory meetings and allowing the employee to respond. The absence of a documented contemporaneous disciplinary pathway and reliance on delayed diary entries undermined procedural fairness.
- Strategic timing : The tribunal considered motive and context including the owner’s reorganisation and the sister’s permanent relocation to the UK. The judge found those factors potentially relevant to the timing of dismissal soon before the employee reached two years continuous service where unfair dismissal protections would become available.
- Precedential value : The decision reaffirms that monitoring alone does not automatically legitimise a dismissal and that how employers document and apply policies matters in tribunal review.

## Rewritten narrative

In a UK employment tribunal decision the judge concluded that limited personal online activity at work does not automatically justify dismissal. Accountancy administrator Ms A Lanuszka was dismissed after an employer installed spyware and recorded 1 hour and 24 minutes of personal use across 13 and 14 July 2023. Employment Judge Michael Magee found that a substantial portion of the recorded time related to professional development such as Excel training and that the employer did not present a clear contemporaneous policy forbidding personal use. The employer relied on diary notes created in 2024 to describe earlier performance issues. The tribunal held those notes were not sufficiently reliable. The timing of dismissal shortly before the employee would reach statutory protection under the two-year-rule and the employer’s inconsistent practice of personal use reduced the justification for summary dismissal. The tribunal awarded Ms Lanuszka more than 14,000 pounds for unfair dismissal and stressed the need for proportionate monitoring, transparent policy and contemporaneous documentation.
